\begin{abstract}
A transit operator that opens a new route has little or no delay history for
it. We study how well tabular models predict arrival delays on such routes,
using \Events{} stop-level events from nine Sydney ferry routes, and how the
evaluation split changes the conclusions. We compare XGBoost, LightGBM and the
tabular foundation model TabPFN, all with default settings, against four
simple baselines. First, the accuracy of the gradient-boosted models falls as
the split becomes stricter, from an $R^2$ of \resm{random}{XGBoost} on a random
split to \resm{trip}{XGBoost} with trips kept together and \resm{time}{XGBoost}
on later dates, while TabPFN, restricted to 10{,}000 training rows, stays near
\resm{trip}{TabPFN} on all three. On routes absent from training no model is
reliably better than predicting a mean delay. Second, TabPFN becomes useful
on a new route from about 100 observed events (mean $R^2$
\fs{pooled}{TabPFN}{100}); the boosted models need about 500 and remain behind
up to 5{,}000. Third, at default settings and on this dataset, TabPFN is
ahead of both boosted models whenever all three are given the same training
rows: on later dates its $R^2$ is \rowresm{time10k}{TabPFN},
\rowresm{time50k}{TabPFN} and \rowresm{timeall}{TabPFN} with 10{,}000,
50{,}000 and all rows, against at most \rowresm{timeall}{LightGBM} for the
boosted models, at the cost of minutes rather than a fraction of a second
per prediction batch. Its advantage at 10{,}000 rows is consistent with
earlier small-data results and is what makes it suited to new routes.

\section{Introduction}
Arrival-delay predictions inform passengers and help operators plan. Models
for this task are trained on the delay history of the routes they serve. A new
or changed route has little or no history, and it is not obvious how much a
model trained on other routes can offer, or how many observations of the new
route are needed before a model is useful. In earlier work we predicted ferry
travel times and delays from open data with machine
learning~\cite{sarhani2023ferries,sarhani2025ferry}. \todo{Add further
verified references on delay prediction in public transport.}

How a model is evaluated determines what such a study can conclude. When
training and test records are separated at random, records of the same
sailing, day and route fall on both sides, and the score describes
interpolation within known services, not prediction for a new
situation~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage,roberts2017crossvalidation}.

We study both questions on stop-level arrival records of nine Sydney ferry
routes, comparing XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost},
LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, the tabular foundation model
TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn,jaeger2026tabpfn35} and four simple
baselines, all with default settings. To our knowledge this is the first
study of ferry delay prediction for routes with little or no history. We
contribute (1) an evaluation ladder of five splits of increasing strictness,
ending with routes absent from training; (2) a few-shot experiment that
measures accuracy on a new route against the number of events observed on
it; and (3) a comparison of the models on identical training rows, from
10{,}000 to all rows, with its cost in prediction time. The lead of TabPFN at
10{,}000 rows is consistent with earlier small-data
results~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} and is not claimed as new. All results
hold for default settings; no model was tuned.

\paragraph{Ferry delay prediction.}
The closest prior work is our
own~\cite{sarhani2023ferries,sarhani2025ferry}, which predicts and analyses
ferry travel times and delays from open data with machine learning.

\paragraph{Tabular models.}
Gradient-boosted trees~\cite{chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm} are a standard
choice for tabular prediction and have been found to match or outperform deep
learning on typical tabular
datasets~\cite{grinsztajn2022why,shwartzziv2022tabular}.
TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} is a tabular foundation model that predicts
from a training set given as context, without dataset-specific training; it
was introduced for datasets of up to about 10{,}000 samples, and the version
we use~\cite{jaeger2026tabpfn35} accepts larger ones.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Leakage is a known cause of optimistic and irreproducible
results~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}. For data with temporal,
spatial or hierarchical structure, splits that respect the structure are
recommended~\cite{roberts2017crossvalidation}; distribution shift in tabular
data has been benchmarked by TableShift~\cite{gardner2023tableshift}.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}
The dataset contains stop-level arrival records for Sydney Ferries, each
joined with the weather reading for the departure hour. \todo{State the
provenance of the ferry records and weather readings.} The file has
\RawRows{} rows. One ferry event (trip identifier, date, stop sequence, stop)
can appear on several rows because an hour can carry several weather
readings; the copies differ almost only in the weather columns. We keep one
row per event, with the mean of the numeric weather columns and the first
copy's value otherwise, which gives \Events{} events.

The events cover \NRoutes{} routes, \NStops{} stops and \NVehicles{} vessels
on \DistinctDates{} dates between \DateMin{} and \DateMax{}, with no records
for May to July 2022. The routes are very unequal: \LargestRoute{} has
\LargestRouteShare\% of the events and \SmallestRoute{} has
\SmallestRouteShare\%, and the mean delay per route ranges from
\EarliestRouteDelay{}~s (\EarliestRoute{}) to \LatestRouteDelay{}~s
(\LatestRoute{}). A trip identifier denotes a scheduled service, not a single
sailing: \TripsSeveralDatesPct\% of the \NTrips{} identifiers occur on more
than one date.

The target is the arrival delay in seconds, clipped to
[\WinsorLow{}, \WinsorHigh{}], the 1st and 99th percentiles of the raw
values; after clipping it has mean \TargetMean{}~s and standard deviation
\TargetStd{}~s. The models use 21 features: six categorical (route, stop,
vessel, wind direction, weather condition, vessel class), twelve numeric
(start time, days since the first date, month, day of year, day of week,
stop sequence, dew point, humidity, wind speed, pressure, seating capacity
and a binary calendar flag named \texttt{Holidays}) and three indicators for
a missing stop, wind direction or condition. \todo{Confirm the meaning of
\texttt{Holidays}: it equals 1 on about 70\% of dates.} The trip identifier
is used only to form splits. Monthly ridership is in the file but is not
used, because it would not be known when a prediction is made.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\paragraph{Models.}
XGBoost and LightGBM use the same fixed settings throughout (300 trees,
learning rate 0.1, row and column subsampling 0.9, depth 6 or 31 leaves).
TabPFN is called through its hosted API with default settings; all TabPFN
results reported here were obtained on 9 and 10 October 2026, when the service
served model version \ServedModel{}~\cite{jaeger2026tabpfn35}. Categorical
features are ordinal-encoded. No hyperparameter is tuned.

\paragraph{Training rows given to TabPFN.}
Unless stated otherwise, we restricted TabPFN to a random 10{,}000-row sample
of the training set; this is our choice, as the service accepted up to
1{,}000{,}000 rows. XGBoost and LightGBM use every training row and are also
trained on exactly TabPFN's rows (the \emph{equal-data} comparison). For
Table~\ref{tab:rows}, all three models are run on identical rows: random
50{,}000-row samples and all rows of the time split, and all rows of the
trip-grouped split, each scored on \FixedTestCap{} fixed test events. The
few-shot experiments keep the 10{,}000-row restriction.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Four baselines use the training rows only: the global mean delay; the mean
per stop; the mean per route and stop, falling back to the stop and then the
global mean for unseen values; and Ridge
regression~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit} on one-hot categorical and standardised
numeric features.

\paragraph{Splits.}
\emph{Random}: 80\% of events for training, 20\% for testing.
\emph{Trip-grouped}: 80\% of trip identifiers for training, so that no
scheduled service is on both sides; \TripSeeds{} seeds. Every test event
still has events of the same date in training.
\emph{Date-grouped}: 20\% of calendar dates held out at random, so that no
date is on both sides; two seeds.
\emph{Time}: training on the earliest 80\% of dates and testing on the latest
20\%. Events after \CutoffDate{} are removed for this split only, because the
file has later records for one route alone. This leaves \TimeTrainRows{}
training events up to \TimeTrainLast{}.
\emph{Unseen routes}: two of the nine routes held out, the other seven used
for training; five choices of held-out routes.
On the date-grouped and time splits every model is scored on the same random
sample of \FixedTestCap{} test events; the other splits are scored on their
full test sets.

\paragraph{Few-shot route experiment.}
Each route in turn is treated as new. Its trip identifiers are divided into
an adaptation pool (80\%) and a test set (20\%), from which a fixed sample of
\FewshotTestCap{} events is scored. For $k \in \{0, 100, 500, 1000, 5000\}$
we draw $k$ events from the pool one trip identifier at a time, since
observations of a new route arrive service by service; a given $k$ therefore
covers different numbers of services on different routes. Each setting is
repeated with \FewshotSeeds{} draws. In the \emph{pooled} condition a model
is trained on all events of the other eight routes plus the $k$ events; in
the \emph{target-only} condition on the $k$ events alone. In the pooled
condition TabPFN receives the $k$ events and a random sample of other-route
events, 10{,}000 rows in total, and the boosted models are also trained on
exactly these rows.

\paragraph{Metric.}
We report $R^2$, computed on the test set at hand: a value below zero is
worse than predicting the mean delay of that test set. Repeated settings are
given as mean $\pm$ standard deviation.

\paragraph{On the same rows TabPFN is ahead at every size.}
Table~\ref{tab:rows} compares the models on identical training rows. At
default settings and on this dataset, TabPFN is ahead of both boosted models
on the time split at 10{,}000 rows (\rowres{time10k}{TabPFN} against
\rowres{time10k}{XGBoost} and \rowres{time10k}{LightGBM}), at 50{,}000
(\rowres{time50k}{TabPFN} against \rowres{time50k}{XGBoost} and
\rowres{time50k}{LightGBM}) and with all rows (\rowres{time10k}{TabPFN}
rising to \rowres{timeall}{TabPFN}, against \rowres{timeall}{XGBoost} and
\rowres{timeall}{LightGBM}). The all-rows figures on the time split are
single runs. The lead at 10{,}000 rows agrees with the results reported for
TabPFN on small datasets~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}; we do not present it as
new. With 10{,}000 rows TabPFN is already at least as good as the boosted
models with all rows, \TimeRowRatio{} times more.

\paragraph{With all rows, TabPFN gains most where dates are shared.}
On the trip-grouped split TabPFN leads at 10{,}000 rows
(\rowres{trip10k}{TabPFN} against \rowres{trip10k}{XGBoost} and
\rowres{trip10k}{LightGBM}) and by much more with all rows
(\rowres{tripall}{TabPFN} against \rowres{tripall}{XGBoost} and
\rowres{tripall}{LightGBM}). Going from 10{,}000 to all rows raises its
$R^2$ by \rowgain{trip}{TabPFN} on the trip-grouped split, but only by
\rowgain{date}{TabPFN} on the date-grouped split (to
\rowres{dateall}{TabPFN}) and \rowgain{time}{TabPFN} on the time split. The
trip-grouped split leaves events of the same date as each test event in
training, and the date-grouped split removes exactly that; the likely reason
for the large trip-grouped gain is therefore that TabPFN, given all rows,
exploits same-day events. We did not test this more directly. On the
date-grouped split TabPFN with all rows is still ahead of the boosted models
(\rowres{dateall}{XGBoost} and \rowres{dateall}{LightGBM}). In
Table~\ref{tab:splits} the boosted models are ahead of TabPFN on the
trip-grouped and date-grouped splits only because TabPFN is restricted to
10{,}000 rows there. With all rows the evaluation ladder applies to TabPFN
as well.

\paragraph{The cost is prediction time.}
With all training rows, TabPFN took \rowpred{timeall}{TabPFN}~s (about six
minutes) to predict the \FixedTestCap{} test events of the time split
through the hosted service, against \rowpred{timeall}{XGBoost}~s for XGBoost
and \rowpred{timeall}{LightGBM}~s for LightGBM run locally; with 10{,}000
rows it took \rowpred{time10k}{TabPFN}~s. We have not established why the
boosted models lose more than TabPFN on later dates at 10{,}000 rows:
removing the four date-derived features changed their $R^2$ on the time
split by at most \NoDateMaxChange{}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Data.}
The source file has exactly the row limit of a spreadsheet application and
its last month contains one route only, so it was probably truncated on
export. \todo{Confirm with the data provider.} The weather features are
observed readings; a deployed model would have to use forecasts. The results
rest on nine routes of one operator, which differ strongly in size.

\paragraph{Method.}
No model was tuned, so every comparison holds for default settings and for
this dataset; tuned boosted models and CatBoost were not tested. The time
split uses a single cutoff, and on it the all-rows results are single runs.
The few-shot, unseen-route and random-split results keep TabPFN at 10{,}000
rows, so we do not know how it performs there with more. TabPFN was used
through a hosted service whose default model can change; our results are for
the version served on the date given, and its prediction times include the
network.

\section{Conclusion}
We compared XGBoost, LightGBM and TabPFN, with default settings, for
predicting ferry arrival delays on routes with little or no history. The
accuracy of the boosted models falls with each stricter evaluation split,
and on routes absent from training no model is reliably better than
predicting a mean delay. With about 100 observed events TabPFN becomes
useful on a new route; the boosted models need about 500 and remain behind
up to 5{,}000. At default settings and on this dataset, TabPFN is ahead of
both boosted models on the same training rows at 10{,}000, 50{,}000 and all
rows; the lead at 10{,}000 is what earlier small-data results lead one to
expect, and it comes at the cost of much slower prediction with all rows.
For an operator, a small number of observed services on a new route is
enough to start predicting its delays, provided the model is data-efficient.
Tuned boosted models, CatBoost, forecast weather and other networks are the
natural next steps.
