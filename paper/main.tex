\begin{abstract}
A transit operator that opens a new route has little or no delay history for
it. We study how well tabular models predict arrival delays on such routes,
using \Events{} stop-level events from nine Sydney ferry routes, and how the
choice of evaluation split changes the conclusions. We compare XGBoost,
LightGBM and the tabular foundation model TabPFN, all with default settings,
against four simple baselines. First, the measured accuracy of the
gradient-boosted models falls as the split becomes stricter: $R^2$ of
\resm{random}{XGBoost} on a random split, \resm{trip}{XGBoost} when trips are
kept together, \resm{time}{XGBoost} when testing on later dates, and below zero
for every model on routes absent from training, where no model beats the mean
delay of the training routes. Second, with no events from a new route no
model is useful, but TabPFN becomes useful from about 100 events (mean $R^2$
\fs{pooled}{TabPFN}{100}), whereas
the boosted models need about 500 and remain behind up to 5{,}000. Third,
consistent with earlier small-data results for TabPFN, it is more
data-efficient: given the same 10{,}000 training rows it is ahead of both
boosted models on the trip-grouped and the time split, and on the time split
it is at least as good as boosted models trained on \TimeRowRatio{} times
more rows. This data efficiency is what makes it suited to new routes.

\keywords{Delay prediction \and Public transport \and Tabular foundation
models \and Gradient boosting \and Cold start \and Evaluation protocol}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Arrival-delay predictions inform passengers and help operators plan. Models
for this task are trained on the delay history of the routes they serve. A new
or changed route has little or no such history, and it is not obvious how much
a model trained on other routes can offer, or how many observations of the new
route are needed before a model is useful. Earlier work has predicted ferry
delays from open data with machine learning~\cite{sarhani2025ferry}.
\todo{Add further verified references on delay prediction in public
transport.}

How a model is evaluated determines what such a study can conclude. When
training and test records are separated at random, records of the same trip,
day and route appear on both sides, and the test score describes
interpolation within known services rather than prediction for a new
situation~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage,roberts2017crossvalidation}.

We study both questions on stop-level arrival records of nine Sydney ferry
routes. We compare two gradient-boosted tree models,
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, with the
tabular foundation model TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} and four simple
baselines. All models use default settings. To our knowledge this is the
first study of ferry delay prediction for routes with little or no history.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An evaluation ladder.} We evaluate the same models under four
splits of increasing strictness. For the boosted models $R^2$ falls from
\resm{random}{XGBoost} (random) to \resm{trip}{XGBoost} (trip-grouped) and
\resm{time}{XGBoost} (later dates), and it is below zero for every model when
whole routes are held out: with no events from a route, no model beats the
mean delay of the training routes.
\item \textbf{A few-shot route experiment.} Treating each route in turn as
new, we measure accuracy as a function of the number $k$ of events observed
on it. With $k=0$ every model has an $R^2$ below zero. TabPFN is
better than the route's own mean delay from about 100 events; the boosted
models need about 500 and stay behind TabPFN up to 5{,}000.
\item \textbf{A data-efficiency comparison.} We restrict TabPFN to at most
10{,}000 training rows. Trained on exactly those rows, both boosted models are
behind TabPFN on the trip-grouped and the time split. This is consistent with
the small-data results reported for TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} rather
than a new finding; what we add is that on the time split TabPFN is at least
as good as the boosted models trained on \TimeRowRatio{} times more rows.
\end{enumerate}
We read the three results together: TabPFN's data efficiency is what makes it
suited to routes with little history. All results hold for default settings;
no model was tuned.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Tabular models.}
Gradient-boosted decision trees such as XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and
LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} are a standard choice for tabular prediction.
Benchmark studies have found them to match or outperform deep learning models
on typical tabular datasets~\cite{grinsztajn2022why,shwartzziv2022tabular}.
TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} is a tabular foundation model that predicts
from a training set given as context, without dataset-specific training, and
was introduced for datasets of up to about 10{,}000 samples.

\paragraph{Evaluation and distribution shift.}
Leakage, in which information that would not be available at prediction time
enters training or evaluation, is a known cause of optimistic
results~\cite{kaufman2012leakage} and of irreproducible findings across
fields~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}. For data with temporal, spatial or
hierarchical structure, random cross-validation underestimates prediction
error, and splits that respect the structure are
recommended~\cite{roberts2017crossvalidation}. Distribution shift in tabular
data has been benchmarked by TableShift~\cite{gardner2023tableshift}.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source}
The dataset contains stop-level arrival records for Sydney Ferries, each joined
with the weather reading for the departure hour. \todo{State the provenance of
the ferry records and of the weather readings, and who compiled the file.} The
file has \RawRows{} rows and \RawColumns{} columns. Each row gives the trip
identifier, the route, the scheduled start time and date, the position of the
stop within the trip, the stop, the vessel, the arrival delay in seconds
(positive when late), and the weather: dew point, humidity, wind direction,
wind speed, pressure and a text condition.

\subsection{De-duplication}
One ferry event, identified by trip identifier, start date, stop sequence and
stop, can appear on several rows, because an hour can carry more than one
weather reading. Of the \Events{} distinct events, \MultiCopyEvents{} appear
more than once, up to \MaxCopies{} times. The copies of an event differ almost
only in the weather columns; the arrival delay differs between copies in
\DelayConflictEvents{} events. We keep one row per event, with the mean of the
four numeric weather columns and the first copy's value for every other
column. All experiments use these \Events{} events.

The events cover \NRoutes{} routes, \NStops{} stops and \NVehicles{} vessels,
on \DistinctDates{} dates between \DateMin{} and \DateMax{}. There are no
records for May, June and July 2022. A trip identifier denotes a scheduled
service, not a single sailing: \TripsSeveralDatesPct\% of the \NTrips{} trip
identifiers occur on more than one date, up to \MaxDatesPerTrip{}, giving
\NSailings{} sailings in total. Table~\ref{tab:routes} gives the size of each
route. The routes differ in size, in the number of events per trip identifier,
and in their typical delay.

\subsection{Cleaning and Target}
\label{sec:cleaning}
Weather values stored as text with units are converted to numbers. The start
time is expressed in minutes after midnight. The raw arrival delay has extreme
values, up to \RawDelayMax{}~s, so it is clipped to the interval
[\WinsorLow{}, \WinsorHigh{}]~s, the 1st and 99th percentiles of the raw
values. This changes \ClippedLow{} events at the lower end and \ClippedHigh{}
at the upper end. After clipping, the target has mean \TargetMean{}~s and
standard deviation \TargetStd{}~s.

Missing values are not imputed for the tree-based models and TabPFN. Three
indicator features record whether the stop, the wind direction or the weather
condition is missing; a missing wind direction or condition becomes its own
category. The stop is missing in \MissingStopEvents{} events.

\subsection{Features}
The models use 21 features: six categorical (route, stop, vessel, wind
direction, weather condition, vessel class), twelve numeric (start time, days
since the first date, month, day of year, day of week, stop sequence, dew
point, humidity, wind speed, pressure, seating capacity, and a binary calendar
flag named \texttt{Holidays} in the file) and the three missing-value
indicators. \todo{Confirm the meaning of the \texttt{Holidays} flag with the
data provider: it equals 1 on about 70\% of dates, so it may mark ordinary
days rather than holidays.} The trip identifier is used only to form splits.

Five columns of the file are not used. Precipitation is constant. The hour
duplicates the start time. Wind gust is zero on most rows. Total capacity is
fixed per vessel and closely follows seating capacity. Ridership is a monthly
total per route, which would not be known when a prediction is made.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Models}
We compare XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} and
TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}. No hyperparameter is tuned for any model.
XGBoost and LightGBM use the same fixed settings in every experiment: 300
trees, learning rate 0.1, row and column subsampling of 0.9, and a maximum
depth of 6 (XGBoost) or 31 leaves (LightGBM). TabPFN is used through its
hosted API with default settings and the service's default model (client
library tabpfn-client 0.4.1); the model versions are given in the
reproducibility note at the end of the paper. \todo{Cite the reference for
the model version served (v3.5); \cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} describes an
earlier version.} Categorical features are ordinal-encoded for these three
models, and a category not seen in training receives a separate code.

\paragraph{Training rows given to TabPFN.}
We restricted TabPFN to a random 10{,}000-row sample of the training set
whenever the training set is larger. This is our choice, not a limit of the
service, which accepted up to 1{,}000{,}000 training rows for its default
model when we queried it on 9 October 2026. It keeps the number of rows sent
to the hosted service small, and 10{,}000 rows is the dataset size for which
TabPFN was introduced~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}. We did not run TabPFN on
more rows. XGBoost and LightGBM use every training row. To separate the
effect of the model from the effect of the amount of data, we also train
XGBoost and LightGBM on exactly the 10{,}000 rows that TabPFN receives. We
refer to this as the equal-data comparison.

\subsection{Baselines}
Four baselines use statistics of the training rows only:
(a)~the mean delay of all training rows;
(b)~the mean delay per stop;
(c)~the mean delay per route and stop, falling back to (b) for a route and
stop pair not seen in training, and to (a) for a stop not seen in training;
(d)~Ridge regression~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit} with default regularisation on
one-hot encoded categorical features and standardised numeric features, with
missing numeric values replaced by the training median.

\subsection{Splits}
We evaluate under four splits of the same events.
\begin{description}
\item[Random.] 80\% of events for training and 20\% for testing, chosen at
random. Stops of the same sailing can fall on both sides.
\item[Trip-grouped.] 80\% of trip identifiers for training and 20\% for
testing, so that no scheduled service is on both sides. Repeated with
\TripSeeds{} random seeds.
\item[Unseen routes.] Two of the nine routes are held out for testing and the
other seven are used for training. Repeated for five random choices of the
held-out routes.
\item[Time.] Training on the earliest 80\% of calendar dates and testing on the
latest 20\%, with no date on both sides. Events after \CutoffDate{} are removed
for this split only (\EventsAfterCutoff{} events), because the file contains
later records for a single route and the test period should cover all nine.
This leaves \TimeTrainRows{} training events on \TimeTrainDates{} dates up to
\TimeTrainLast{}, and a test period of \TimeTestDates{} dates from
\TimeTestFirst{} to \CutoffDate{}. The split is deterministic.
\end{description}

On the random, trip-grouped and unseen-route splits, models are scored on the
full test set. On the time split, every model is scored on the same fixed
random sample of \FixedTestCap{} of the \TimeTestPeriodRows{} test-period
events. On the time split TabPFN is run on \TimeTabRuns{} different random
10{,}000-row samples of the training events; XGBoost and LightGBM are run once
on all training events and once on each of the samples.

\subsection{Few-Shot Route Experiment}
\label{sec:fewshot-protocol}
This experiment measures how accuracy on a new route grows with the number of
events observed on it. Each of the nine routes in turn is treated as the new
route. Its trip identifiers are divided into an adaptation pool (80\%) and a
test set (20\%); the test set is a fixed sample of \FewshotTestCap{} events,
the same for every model and setting.

For $k \in \{0, 100, 500, 1000, 5000\}$, we draw $k$ events from the
adaptation pool one trip identifier at a time: all events of randomly chosen
trip identifiers are added, and the last one is cut after its first stops so
that exactly $k$ events are used. Observations of a new route arrive service
by service rather than as isolated events, and this sampling reflects that.
Because routes differ in events per trip identifier, a given $k$ covers
different numbers of trip identifiers on different routes. Each setting is
repeated with \FewshotSeeds{} random draws.

Models are trained under two conditions:
\begin{description}
\item[Pooled.] All events of the other eight routes, plus the $k$ events of
the new route. With $k=0$ the model has never seen the new route.
\item[Target-only.] The $k$ events of the new route alone ($k>0$).
\end{description}
In the pooled condition the training set always exceeds 10{,}000 rows. TabPFN
then receives all $k$ events of the new route and a random sample of events
from the other routes, 10{,}000 rows in total. For the equal-data comparison,
XGBoost and LightGBM are also trained on exactly these rows. The four
baselines are evaluated at every $k$ under both conditions.

\paragraph{Time-respecting variant.}
In the experiment above, adaptation and test events of a route are drawn from
the same period. As a robustness check we repeat it with the cutoff of the
time split. For each route, the adaptation events are the $k$ most recent
events of that route up to \TimeTrainLast{}, taken sailing by sailing from the
latest backwards; the test set is a fixed sample of \FewshotTestCap{} events
of that route from the test period; and events of the other routes are taken
only from before the cutoff. The adaptation events are fixed by the calendar,
so each setting is run once.

\subsection{Metrics}
We report the coefficient of determination ($R^2$), the root mean squared
error (RMSE) and the mean absolute error (MAE), the last two in seconds.
$R^2$ is computed on the test set at hand, so a value below zero means that
the model is worse than predicting the mean delay of that test set. Where a
setting is repeated, we report the mean and the standard deviation over the
repetitions.

\paragraph{No model generalises to unseen routes.}
On this split $R^2$ is measured against the mean delay of the held-out events
themselves, that is, of the two held-out routes together. A model with
$R^2=0$ does as well as someone who knew that mean in advance, and a negative
value is worse than that. The mean delay of the seven training routes is a
different number, which is why this baseline also scores slightly below zero
(\res{route}{GlobalMean}). Every model has a mean $R^2$ below zero. The
boosted models are far below (\res{route}{XGBoost} and \res{route}{LightGBM}),
TabPFN less so (\res{route}{TabPFN}), and none is better than the mean delay
of the training routes. The spread across the five choices of held-out routes
is large. In the few-shot experiment below, $R^2$ is measured against the new
route's own mean delay on its test events.

\paragraph{On equal data TabPFN is ahead of both boosted models.}
On the trip-grouped split the boosted models, trained on about \TripRowRatio{}
times more rows than TabPFN, are ahead of it. Trained on exactly TabPFN's
10{,}000 rows they fall behind it, to \res{trip}{XGBoost10k} and
\res{trip}{LightGBM10k} against \res{trip}{TabPFN}. This agrees with the
results reported for TabPFN on datasets of up to 10{,}000
samples~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}; we do not present it as new. On the time
split the
order on equal data is the same (\res{time}{XGBoost10k} and
\res{time}{LightGBM10k} against \res{time}{TabPFN}), and here the boosted
models have no lead with all the data either: TabPFN's \TimeTabRuns{} runs
range from \TimeTabMin{} to \TimeTabMax{}, against \res{time}{XGBoost} and
\res{time}{LightGBM} for the boosted models trained on \TimeRowRatio{} times
more rows. The boosted models were run once on the full training set of this
split, so we describe TabPFN as at least as good, not better. We have not
established why the boosted models' lead disappears on the time split.
Removing the four date-derived features (days since the first date, day of
year, month and day of week) changed the $R^2$ of XGBoost, LightGBM and Ridge
by at most \NoDateMaxChange{}, on all training rows and on 10{,}000, so these

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{The source file may be incomplete.}
The file has \RawRows{} rows, which is the row limit of a spreadsheet
application, and its last month contains records for one route only. It was
probably truncated when it was exported. \todo{Confirm with the data provider
whether the file is complete.} The records after \CutoffDate{} are excluded
from the time split for this reason, and there are no records for three months
of 2022.

\paragraph{Weather is observed, not forecast.}
The weather features are the readings for the departure hour. A deployed model
would have to use forecasts, so the results overstate what weather can
contribute in practice.

\paragraph{Default settings only.}
No model was tuned. The comparison between TabPFN and the gradient-boosted
models therefore holds for the default settings used here; boosted models
tuned for small training sets were not tested.

\paragraph{One network, nine routes.}
The unseen-route and few-shot results rest on nine routes of one operator, and
the routes differ strongly in size. A given $k$ covers different numbers of
trip identifiers on different routes.

\paragraph{One time cutoff.}
The time split uses a single boundary between training and test dates. On
this split XGBoost and LightGBM were run once on all training events, so their
full-data results have no measure of spread.

\paragraph{TabPFN was restricted to a sample.}
We gave TabPFN at most 10{,}000 training rows, although the hosted service
accepts more. Where the training set is larger, its result depends on which
rows are sampled, and we do not know how it performs with more rows.

\paragraph{Two dates, possibly two model versions.}
The TabPFN results were obtained through a hosted service on two occasions,
in August and in October 2026, and the model version served in August was not
recorded. Comparisons between TabPFN's results on the time split or in the
few-shot experiments and its results on the other three splits may therefore
involve different model versions.

\section{Conclusion}
We compared XGBoost, LightGBM and TabPFN, with default settings, for
predicting ferry arrival delays, with the aim of learning what can be expected
on a route with little or no history. Three findings stand out. The measured
accuracy of the boosted models falls with each stricter evaluation split, and
on routes absent from training every model is worse than predicting a mean
delay. With no events from a new route, no model is useful; TabPFN becomes
useful from about 100 events, and the boosted models need about 500 and
remain behind it up to 5{,}000. Given the same training rows TabPFN is ahead
of both boosted models, as earlier small-data results for TabPFN lead one to
expect, and on the time split it is at least as good as
boosted models trained on \TimeRowRatio{} times more rows. For an operator,
the practical reading is that a small number of observed services on a new
route is enough to start predicting its delays, provided the model is
data-efficient. These conclusions are limited to default settings, one ferry
network and one source file that may be incomplete. Tuned boosted models,
forecast weather and other networks are the natural next steps.
